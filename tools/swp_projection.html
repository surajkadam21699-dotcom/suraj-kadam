Why the corpus survives

At 45 the ₹5 crore has grown to ₹8.24 crore untouched. The first year's withdrawal of ₹60 lakh is 7.28% of that, against an assumed 10.5% return.

The portfolio earns more than is taken out, so it keeps compounding through retirement — slowly at first, then steeply, because the gap between what it earns and what is withdrawn widens every year while the withdrawal stays fixed.

The catch: ₹5 lakh does not stay ₹5 lakh

The green line assumes the withdrawal is never increased. At 6% inflation, ₹5 lakh a month in 30 years buys what ₹87,000 buys today — a loss of 83% of its purchasing power.

Raise the withdrawal to keep pace and the picture changes. At 5% a year the corpus just lasts to 70 with nothing left. At 6% it is exhausted at 67. On these assumptions the plan supports roughly a 5% annual increase, not 6%.

Two things that move the answer. This is a pre-tax illustration: each SWP instalment is part capital and part gain, and the gain portion attracts capital gains tax, so ₹15 crore of withdrawals will not reach the bank in full. And the monthly compounding convention matters — 10.5% a year is applied here as a true 10.5%. Many calculators divide by twelve instead, which compounds to 11.02% a year and would show ₹42.82 crore rather than ₹33.32 crore for the same inputs.
