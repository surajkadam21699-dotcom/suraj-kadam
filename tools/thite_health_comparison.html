In health insurance the first rupee of cover is always the most expensive. Doubling the sum insured adds ₹12,719 a year, or 27.8% — not another 100%. That is ₹1,060 a month for a second ₹10 lakh.

A single cardiac or oncology admission in a Pune or Mumbai private hospital can run past ₹10 lakh on its own. With Sunil at 57, this is the decade in which that stops being hypothetical.

Is the three-year lock worth it?

Paying three years up front costs you the use of the year-two and year-three money. The break-even is the return at which paying annually comes out ahead.

The structure worth pricing

A family floater is rated on the eldest life. Anupama at 53 and Ved at 16 are both being charged at a 57-year-old's rate. That is how floaters work, and for two adults close in age it is usually still the cheaper structure.

Ved is the exception. At 16 an individual policy for him costs a fraction of an adult premium. It is worth asking Star Health to quote Sunil and Anupama on a floater, with Ved on his own policy, against all three together.

Beyond price, a separate policy starts Ved's own waiting periods and no-claim bonus now, on a policy he keeps for life — rather than having them begin at 25 when he leaves the family floater.

Why the timing matters at 57

Pre-existing conditions carry a waiting period of up to three years. Starting that clock now means it is fully served before Sunil turns 60 — ahead of the years in which a claim becomes materially more likely.

Every year of delay pushes the end of that waiting period deeper into the decade where it is most likely to be needed, and does so at a higher entry age.

This is the one part of the decision that cannot be recovered later. The sum insured can be raised at renewal; a waiting period can only start when the policy does.

What to ask Star Health for next. Three quotes that would settle the structure: Sunil and Anupama as a two-member floater at ₹20 lakh; Ved as an individual policy at ₹10 lakh; and all three together at ₹20 lakh, which is what is quoted overleaf. Comparing the first two added together against the third decides whether splitting is worth it.

Before you buy

  • Declare existing conditions in full, for all three. Nothing else on these two pages matters if a claim is later rejected for non-disclosure. If Sunil or Anupama has any condition under treatment, say so now and let it be loaded or excluded openly.
  • Check whether these premiums include GST. A quote excluding it changes the comparison materially, and the tax treatment of health premiums has moved in recent years.
  • Confirm the waiting periods and room-rent terms for pre-existing conditions and specific treatments, and whether the plan carries a room-rent or co-payment cap. These decide what you actually recover, far more than the premium difference does.
  • The three-year premium is not fully deductible in year one. Under Section 80D a multi-year premium is allowed proportionately across the years it covers, so paying up front brings no tax advantage — and 80D applies only under the old tax regime.
  • A three-year lock cuts both ways. It fixes the premium against mid-term rate revisions, which matters with Sunil approaching 60. It also prevents porting to a better or cheaper insurer for three years.
  • Count any employer group cover as temporary. It ends with the job and cannot be ported on the same terms, so it should not reduce the sum insured you buy privately.
